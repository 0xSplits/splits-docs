# How we secure our assets

We've spent a lot of time thinking through our team's financial opsec. It's important that we're able to securely store our assets without losing the ability for team members to access them when and where they're needed.

And it's not just money we need to store. Today we hold our company ENS (splits.eth) and recovery keys for our Farcaster account [(@splits](https://warpcast.com/splits)), and we believe that in the future there will be more non-financial assets our company owns.

In this article we're going to outline how we secure our assets at a high level, and then zoom in and discuss each specific account's security profile in detail.

![](/images/how-we-secure-our-assets/img-1.png)

# Our system

As of writing, we have 5 shared accounts for our team. Each account is a multisig (i.e. multiple signatures/approvals are necessary for outgoing transactions, though notably most of our accounts only require 1 approval) that can be used on any EVM network at the same address.

All of these accounts are secured using passkeys. This allows us to retain full sovereignty over our assets without compromising on day-to-day usability. It also means that team members don't need to manage their own seed phrase or install wallet extensions. Our opsec is no longer only as secure as the weakest link's ability to secure a seed phrase.

Across our accounts, we hold about \~\$800k in assets—a mix of stables, ETH, project tokens (donations from OP, UNI, etc), and NFTs.

We currently have the following accounts: Treasury, Operating, Payroll, Eng Testing, and TWIF. Treasury is where the bulk of our assets are held. Operating is for day-to-day general expenses. Eng Testing is a shared account for all engineers, Payroll is self explanatory, and TWIF is a growth experiment we're running.

At the [root](https://www.pcmag.com/encyclopedia/term/root-level#:~:text=The%20highest%20authority%20or%20privilege,user%22%20or%20%22superuser.%22) of all this is our "Recovery" account. This account is unique. It doesn't hold assets and it's not something we interact with day-to-day. It exists for the *sole purpose* of being able to [regain access in the off chance](/accounts/recovery) we lose access to our Treasury. Recovery is a 2-of-3 that uses company-issued hardware wallets as the signers.

The Recovery account/mechanism exists because passkeys are tied to our domain (splits.org), which means if our domain goes down, passkeys become inoperable. So to mitigate this risk, we use traditional Ethereum signers as our escape hatch. This prevent any lock-in risk to our domain.

In summary:

- We have 5 shared accounts that collectively store \$800k in assets across all EVM chains and tokens; we sign transactions on these accounts using passkeys, allowing for full sovereignty (self-custody matters to us) and usability.
- We have a single recovery account, which allows us to regain access to our accounts should we lose access to our day-to-day signers (passkeys).

# Our accounts

Now that you understand the general shape of the system, let's look at each specific account, how it's configured, and how it fits into the system.

![](/images/how-we-secure-our-assets/img-2.png)

## Treasury

This is where we keep the bulk (90%) of our team's assets. These assets are held across 3 networks (Ethereum, Optimism, and Base) in a number of tokens (USDC, ETH, OP, UNI, etc). It also holds our ENS (splits.eth), which resolves to this address, making it the primary revenue-receiving account we have. Funds coming into this account, from any external source, are considered revenue.

Naturally, this account has a higher security threshold than the others. For us, we've provisioned it to be a 2-of-3, meaning there are 3 signers and each outbound transaction requires 2 approvals. All 3 signers are company-issued [YubiKeys](https://www.yubico.com/products/yubikey-5-overview/) held by different, senior people.

This means that no one person has unilateral access to these funds. It also means that if one person were to lose their YubiKey, the other 2 could sign a transaction to swap out the lost YubiKey with a new one. And if 2 of the YubiKeys were to be lost at once, we can use our recovery signers to reset things.

## Operating

As the name suggests, this is our day-to-day shared operating account. It stores most of the funds not held in Primary. Right now, that's about \$25k. We use this account to fund our other accounts, and to cover any day-to-day expenses that don't fit into the other buckets (e.g. security audits, bug bounties, event sponsoring, etc).

This account has the same signers as Treasury, but only one signature is needed to approve a transaction. This lowers the friction involved for making smaller payments. We require people to add transactions memos, so that we understand where funds are going and why. If one of the signers rage quit or leaked, the source would be legible to the whole team—I can see it was Will's iCloud passkey that approved a transaction at 2:35pm.

## Payroll

We use this account to pay contractors onchain. Keeping this separate from Operating allows us to automatically categorize the expenses in our accounting software and grant more limited signing privileges to a part time EA that helps with internal operations.

We keep the balance in this account limited to no more than a month of future payments, and top it up regularly out of Operating.

## Eng Testing

Every engineer has access to funds in this account for testing. We keep about \$1k in here, which is far more than any engineer needs to test with, and also means we rarely need to top it up. Our goal with this account is to keep engineers unblocked in their day-to-day efforts, while limiting the downside scenario in case of a leak or rage-quit. These outbound transactions are also automatically categorized as R&D by our accounting software.

## TWIF

This stands for *This Week in Farcaster*, which is [a content/media experiment we ran](https://paragraph.xyz/@weekinfarcaster). We have an ENS (weekinfarcaster.eth) and [Farcaster username](https://paragraph.xyz/@weekinfarcaster) tied to this account, from which we published content, interviews, live streams, etc.

We've been working with a talented writer, [Sonya](https://warpcast.com/sonyasupposedly), on this, and having this account is an easy way to carve out a portion of our assets for her to run marketing experiments. As such, we have accounting rules in place to categorize these transactions.

We've found this setup to work really well because it doesn't require Sonya to "bring her own wallet" to our team, nor does it require us to lose access to the funds we've allocated to this experiment, since both Will and I are signers on the account and the balance is included in our "total assets" on our dashboard. All the while, Sonya can sign transactions on this account using tools she's familiar with—her face or fingerprint.

## Onboarding

Not shown in the diagrams above, this is a temporary account we keep a few hundred dollars in. Right now we're [onboarding folks to Splits manually](https://splits.org/teams/), and as part of that process we send a few dollars to their account immediately upon creation so they can experience using the product immediately.

Right now there are just 3 of us doing these manual onboardings, so this account is a 1-of-3, giving any one of us access to these funds whenever needed. Similar to the other smaller accounts, we have accounting rules configured here as well.

When we stop onboarding users manually and decide to close this account, it'll take 10 seconds to sweep out all funds and archive it from our team.
